# Bridging: connecting the topic to its past and to neighbours

Goal: situate the main topic in a wider landscape while the reader always knows which topic the text is about.

## Principles
1. **Direction of travel is always toward the main topic.** Go from the neighbour to the topic, never the reverse and never stay in the neighbour.
2. **Name the relationship.** Pick one: precedes, builds on, generalises, specialises, contrasts with, overlaps with, enables, competes with, is applied in.
3. **Be short.** A neighbouring topic gets a paragraph or two, not a section of its own in the core of the survey.
4. **Past before present.** When a topic sits between two others, present them in the order of their relation to it (e.g. the older one first), then land on the main topic.
5. **Budget.** Keep context under roughly one third of the body.

## Patterns

**Historical antecedent**
"Before [topic], the dominant approach was [past topic], which [limitation]. [Topic] emerged to address this by [key idea]."

**Topic between two others (A and B)**
"[A] focuses on [aspect], while [B] focuses on [aspect]. [Topic] sits between them: it takes [element] from [A] and [element] from [B], which allows [distinct contribution]."

**Contrast**
"[Neighbour] tackles a similar problem but differs in [dimension]. This difference matters for [topic] because [reason]."

**Enabling / applied-in**
"Advances in [neighbour] made [topic] practical by [mechanism]. Conversely, [topic] feeds back into [neighbour] through [mechanism]."

**Return sentence (end every context paragraph with one)**
"With this context in place, the rest of the survey returns to [topic] and examines..."

## Warning signs
- A context subsection longer than any core subsection.
- A neighbour introduced with no stated relationship.
- Headings that name the neighbour as if it were the subject.
- The conclusion talking more about neighbours than about the main topic.
